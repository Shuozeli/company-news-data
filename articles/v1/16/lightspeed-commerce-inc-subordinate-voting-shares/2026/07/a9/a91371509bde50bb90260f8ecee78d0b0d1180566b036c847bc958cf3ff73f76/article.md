Inventory prices aren’t static, and their value can change depending on several factors in the market. When inventory values go down, businesses need to adjust their financial records to reflect the true worth of their stock.


This is where inventory write-downs come in.


For retailers, write-downs aren’t unusual. Stock spread across multiple locations or channels can lose value for many reasons—obsolescence, damage or even sudden market changes. These losses don’t just affect your inventory; they cut into your profitability and cash flow.


Knowing why inventory write-downs happen, how to calculate them, and what they mean for your business is critical. It helps you manage stock strategically and avoid unnecessary financial hits.

## Key takeaways


- An inventory write-down occurs when inventory loses value but still retains some recoverable worth. Unlike a write-off, the inventory is not removed entirely from the books.


- Common causes of an inventory write-down include damage, spoilage, obsolescence, declining market demand, excess inventory, and changes in consumer preferences.


- A write down of inventory reduces the reported value of inventory on the balance sheet and is recognized as an expense on the income statement.


- Timely inventory write-downs help businesses maintain accurate financial records and ensure inventory is valued realistically.


- Delaying or failing to record inventory write-downs can overstate assets, inflate profits, and create misleading financial statements.


- Regular inventory audits, cycle counts, and inventory performance reviews can help businesses identify inventory that may require a write-down before losses grow larger.


- Strong demand forecasting, inventory optimization, and stock management practices can help reduce the need for future inventory write-downs.


- Monitoring inventory write-down trends can provide valuable insights into purchasing decisions, product performance, and overall inventory health.


## What is an inventory write-down?


An inventory write-down happens when a business lowers the recorded value of its inventory because its market value has dropped below what it originally cost. This adjustment ensures financial records reflect what the inventory is actually worth—whether it’s damaged, expired, or outdated. Unlike a write-off, which removes inventory value entirely, a write-down only reduces it partially.


Write-downs are often needed when inventory can’t be sold at full price anymore. Maybe it’s seasonal stock that’s past its prime selling window. Or perhaps it’s slightly damaged but still sellable—just at a steep discount. Either way, the reduced value must be accounted for to keep financial records accurate.


This process follows the accounting principle of conservatism, where losses are recorded as soon as they’re identified. Write-downs show up as an expense on the income statement, lowering net income, and reducing inventory value on the balance sheet. On the other hand, inventory with no remaining value—like spoiled goods or completely obsolete products—calls for a write-off instead.


Clear and accurate inventory valuation is essential for staying financially transparent. By understanding the difference between write-downs and write-offs, businesses can handle inventory issues strategically and avoid unnecessary financial strain.


## When do inventory write-downs occur?


Inventory write-downs happen when the value of stock drops below what it originally cost. These situations directly affect financial reporting and need quick action to reflect the inventory’s actual value.


### Obsolescence due to changes in consumer demand


Consumer preferences change fast, and inventory often can’t keep up. Last season’s fashion trends or older tech products quickly lose their appeal. When items become outdated, their value drops—meaning you need to write them down to match what they’re worth now.


### Perishability in industries like food or pharmaceuticals


Some products have a ticking clock—like fresh produce or prescription medications. Once they’re close to or past their expiration dates, their value plummets. A grocer sitting on unsold dairy products, for instance, must adjust the inventory’s book value to avoid overstating assets.


### Damage during storage or transportation


Inventory isn’t immune to accidents. Goods can get damaged while stored or during transit—broken glassware, water-damaged fabrics or dented packaging all reduce their value. If the items can’t sell at full price, you’ll need to account for the loss with a write-down.


### Market value declines in fast-changing industries


Industries like tech and fashion are especially prone to sudden price drops. Think of a retailer with last year’s smartphones—they’ll face steep discounts as customers move toward newer models. Or consider seasonal items like winter jackets, which are often heavily marked down to clear shelves. In both cases, a write-down ensures your financial records reflect the real value of what you’re holding.


Identifying these triggers early helps you stay ahead, reduce losses and maintain accurate records.


## **What items are eligible for inventory write-down?**


Inventory may be eligible for a write-down when its market value falls below its original cost but it still retains some recoverable value. In these situations, businesses reduce the inventory’s carrying value to better reflect its current worth rather than removing it from the books entirely.


### Obsolete inventory


One common category is obsolete inventory. This includes products that have become outdated due to:


- Technological advancements


- New product releases


- Changing consumer preferences


- Industry shifts


For example, older electronics or seasonal merchandise may lose value as newer alternatives enter the market.


### Damaged inventory


Damaged inventory may also qualify for a write-down if the products can still be sold at a discounted price. Items that have cosmetic defects, damaged packaging, or minor imperfections often retain some value, even if they can no longer be sold at full price.


### Excess or overstock inventory


Excess or overstock inventory is another common reason for write-downs. When businesses purchase more inventory than demand supports, they may need to discount products significantly to move stock, reducing the inventory’s market value.


### Perishable or slow-moving inventory


Additionally, perishable or slow-moving inventory may require a write-down as products approach expiration dates or experience declining demand. Retailers often mark down these items to encourage sales before they become unsellable.


Regular inventory reviews can help businesses identify inventory that may require a write-down, ensuring inventory values remain accurate and financial statements reflect the true value of inventory on hand.


## How is an inventory write-down calculated?


An inventory write-down starts with finding its net realizable value (NRV). NRV is the expected selling price of an item—less any costs needed to prepare, ship or sell it. The write-down is simply the gap between the inventory’s original value and its NRV.


### Steps to calculate an inventory write-down:


- Evaluate current market value: Look at what the inventory could sell for in its current condition. Consider factors like market trends, damage or obsolescence that might lower its value.


- Subtract costs to sell: Deduct any costs required to make the sale happen. This includes things like packaging, shipping or necessary repairs.


- Compare NRV to original cost: If the NRV is lower than the inventory’s recorded value, the difference is your write-down amount.


### Example calculation:


Let’s say you have 100 units of a seasonal product originally valued at $50 each, for a total of $5,000. Due to declining demand, the selling price drops to $40 per unit, and shipping costs are $5 per unit.


- NRV per unit: $40 (new selling price) – $5 (shipping cost) = $35


- Write-down per unit: $50 (original value) – $35 (NRV) = $15


- Total write-down: 100 units x $15 = $1,500


In this case, you’d reduce the inventory value on your balance sheet by $1,500. It would also appear as an expense on your income statement. By staying on top of your inventory’s value, you can keep your financial records accurate and avoid surprises.


## Impact of inventory write-down on financial statements


An inventory write-down affects multiple areas of a company’s financial statements because it reduces the reported value of inventory. While a write down of inventory helps ensure assets are valued accurately, it can also have a significant impact on profitability and key financial metrics.


### **Impact on income statement**


An inventory write-down is typically recorded as an expense during the accounting period in which the loss occurs. Depending on accounting practices, the write-down may be included in cost of goods sold (COGS) or recorded as a separate inventory-related expense. As a result, the business reports higher expenses and lower earnings for the period.


### **Impact on balance sheet**


When an inventory write-down occurs, the value of inventory recorded on the balance sheet decreases. Because inventory is classified as a current asset, the write-down reduces total current assets and overall asset value. This adjustment helps ensure the balance sheet reflects the inventory’s actual market value rather than its original cost.


### **Impact on gross margin and net profit**


Because an inventory write-down increases expenses, it can reduce both gross margin and net profit. If the write-down is included in COGS, gross profit and gross margin decline as the cost associated with inventory rises. Regardless of where the expense is recorded, net income typically decreases because the business is recognizing a loss in inventory value.


### **Effect on financial ratios**


An inventory write-down can also influence several important financial ratios used to evaluate business performance. Lower inventory values may affect inventory turnover calculations, while reduced profits can impact profitability metrics such as net profit margin and return on assets (ROA). Investors, lenders, and business owners often monitor these ratios closely, making accurate inventory valuation essential for reliable financial analysis and decision-making.

## FAQs on Inventory Write-Down


### Can I write off expired inventory?


You can write off expired inventory if it no longer has resale value. This adjustment removes the inventory from your accounting records by recording the loss as an expense. To stay compliant with accounting standards, keep detailed documentation—like inventory counts and expiration records.


### Is an inventory write-off tax deductible?


Yes, inventory write-offs are usually tax deductible because they’re treated as business expenses. They lower your taxable income for the period when the write-off happens. Just make sure your records are thorough and align with local tax rules in case they’re ever reviewed.


### Is an inventory write-off an expense?


An inventory write-off is always recorded as an expense on your income statement. Smaller write-offs are often included in the


[cost of goods sold (COGS)](https://www.lightspeedhq.com/blog/what-is-cogs/) . Larger write-offs, however, may appear as their own line item to clearly show their impact on your financials.


### **How does inventory write-down affect gross profit?**


An inventory write-down can reduce gross profit by increasing the cost associated with inventory. If the write-down is recorded through cost of goods sold (COGS), gross profit decreases because the business is recognizing a loss in inventory value. As a result, gross margin may also decline, which can affect how profitable the business appears during the reporting period.


### **When should inventory be written down under GAAP?**


Under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), inventory should be written down when its market value falls below its recorded cost. This typically occurs when inventory becomes damaged, obsolete, slow-moving, or less valuable due to changing market conditions. The write-down ensures inventory is reported at the lower of cost or net realizable value (NRV), helping businesses maintain accurate and compliant financial statements.


### **Are inventory write-downs common in retail businesses?**


Yes, inventory write-downs are relatively common in retail businesses. Retailers frequently encounter situations where products lose value due to seasonal changes, shifting consumer demand, overstocking, product damage, or obsolescence. Regular inventory reviews help retailers identify inventory that may require a write-down and ensure inventory values accurately reflect what products can realistically be sold for in the current market.
