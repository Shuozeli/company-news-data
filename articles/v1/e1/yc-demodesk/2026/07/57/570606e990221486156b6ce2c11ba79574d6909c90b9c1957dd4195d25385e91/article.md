# The 6 Conversation Metrics That Matter on Sales Calls (Benchmarks Included)

## TL;DR


Six conversation metrics predict sales call quality: talk ratio (aim for 40/60 in the customer's favor), longest monologue (under 3 minutes), longest customer story, engagement (5+ speaker switches per 5-minute interval), question rate (18+ per hour), and talking speed (140-160 words per minute).[Conversation intelligence](https://demodesk.com/conversation-intelligence) tools measure all six automatically on every call.


## What are conversation metrics?


Conversation metrics quantify how a sales call went, independent of what was said. They measure the mechanics of the dialogue: who spoke, for how long, how often the conversation changed hands, and how many questions were asked.


They matter because the mechanics are coachable. A rep who talks 75% of the time won't fix it by being told to “listen more.” They fix it when they see the number after every call, next to a target.


Manual call review covers 1-2% of a team's conversations.[Conversation intelligence](https://demodesk.com/conversation-intelligence) measures every call, so the numbers below stop being spot-check anecdotes and become a coaching baseline.


Here are the six metrics worth tracking, with the target ranges we use in Demodesk's default scorecards.


## 1. Talk ratio


**Definition:** the share of total call time each participant speaks. Talk ratio = time the rep spoke ÷ total call time.


**Benchmark:** 40-50% for the rep. GTMnow reports that top-producing B2B sales professionals speak 43% of the time, leaving 57% to the prospect.


**How to read it:** a rep consistently above 60% is presenting, not selling. The customer isn't sharing pain points, which means the rep is pitching blind. A rep far below 40% isn't necessarily doing great discovery either. Sometimes the customer has taken over and the call has no direction.


**How to improve it:** open-ended questions, summarize-then-ask follow-ups, and shorter answers. Most reps over-explain because silence feels risky. It isn't.


## 2. Longest monologue


**Definition:** the longest uninterrupted stretch the rep speaks.


**Benchmark:** under 3 minutes. Past that, a call turns into a lecture.


**How to read it:** long monologues cluster in two places: product pitches and objection responses. Both are exactly the moments where you want the customer engaged, not enduring.


**How to improve it:** break explanations with check-ins (“Does this match how your team works?”). If a rep's demos routinely produce 5-minute monologues, restructure the demo into question-led segments rather than coaching the symptom.


## 3. Longest customer story


**Definition:** the longest uninterrupted stretch the customer speaks.


**Benchmark:** there's no upper limit worth enforcing. What matters is that it exists. A call where the customer never speaks for 60+ seconds straight is a call where nobody asked a question big enough.


**How to read it:** long customer stories are where qualification actually happens: pain, stakeholders, budget context, and urgency surface in stories, not in yes/no answers. If this metric is consistently short across a rep's calls, their discovery questions are too narrow.


**How to improve it:** ask questions that require a story to answer. “Walk me through what happened the last time this broke” beats “Is this a problem for you?”


## 4. Engagement (speaker switches)


**Definition:** how often the conversation changes hands, normalized per 5-minute interval so long calls compare fairly with short ones. Short interjections (“mhm”, “right”) don't count.


**Benchmark:** 5 or more switches per 5-minute interval, roughly one real exchange per minute.


**How to read it:** engagement is the single best proxy for whether a call was a dialogue or a broadcast. It also exposes call structure: a demo section will naturally dip, but a discovery section that dips is a warning sign.


**How to improve it:** prompts that hand the floor back (“How does your team handle that today?”), and resisting the urge to answer your own questions.


## 5. Question rate


**Definition:** questions the rep asks per hour of call time.


**Benchmark:** 18+ per hour.


**How to read it:** a low question rate almost always pairs with a high talk ratio and long monologues. The three metrics fail together, and question rate is usually the root cause: reps who ask enough questions can't dominate a call even if they try.


**How to improve it:** prepared question sets per call type. Not a script, a safety net for when the conversation stalls.


## 6. Talking speed


**Definition:** the rep's pace in words per minute.


**Benchmark:** 140-160 words per minute.


**How to read it:** fast talking usually signals nervousness, and it compounds: the customer stops processing, asks fewer questions, engagement drops. Very slow delivery costs credibility and attention.


**How to improve it:** deliberate pauses after key points. Reps rarely notice their own pace on the call. They notice it immediately when they see the number and replay the recording.

## How to coach with these metrics (without turning them into quotas)


The numbers are signals, not sentences. A 70% talk ratio on a pricing call the customer requested is fine. The same ratio on a first discovery call is a problem. Context decides, which is why the metric review belongs inside coaching conversations, not on a leaderboard.


The practical loop looks like this:


1. **Measure every call automatically.** Demodesk's[AI Coach](https://demodesk.com/ai-sales-coaching) scores each call within seconds of it ending, including all six metrics above, so reps see their numbers while the conversation is still fresh.
2. **Pick one metric per rep per month.** A rep working on talk ratio and question rate and monologue length simultaneously improves none of them.
3. **Review clips, not averages.** The metric finds the moment; the recording shows what to do differently. “Your longest monologue was 6 minutes, minute 14 to 20, let's watch it” is coaching. “Lower your talk ratio” is not.
