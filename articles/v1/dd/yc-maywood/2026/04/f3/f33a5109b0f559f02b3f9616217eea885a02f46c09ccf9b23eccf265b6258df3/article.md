# Winning the Right Conversation

In dealmaking, timing is revenue. Relationship building is everything. You build trust with management teams, buyers, bankers, lawyers, auditors, etc. so that you receive the next call on when a company wants to sell, receiving a first look at a potential investment, and getting looped into a deal process. These relationships require domain expertise, extensive experience in the space, and a track record of excellence.


So, this begs the question: how do I build these relationships? How can I create an advantage to get in front of opportunities (or even create them) before others do? How can I scale this so that I can significantly scale my book of business, further bringing in more revenue and also deepening my track record?


These questions only become more complicated when we contextualize the scope of these questions. There are roughly 35 million businesses in the US. When broken down by industry, there are 4.5 million professional and tech services companies, 2.9 million retail companies, 2.6 million healthcare companies, 1.3 million industrial companies, 600,000 financial services companies, and 500,000 technology companies. When starting to reach scale, there are still roughly 250,000 companies that generate more than $10 million in revenue.


How can one effectively maintain relationships, know everything in their universe that is happening, and then act accordingly to win deals?


## Indicators of Timing


### Historical


The traditional way to get in front of the right people was straightforward, but extremely unscalable.


First, know the industry better than anyone else. Understand the major players, the emerging companies, the ownership map, the consolidators, the disruptive trends, and the subtle pressures shaping management behavior.


Second, spend time where the market gathers. Conferences, trade associations, small dinners, board relationships, lender networks, and long-term founder conversations all created informational edge.


Third, build credibility over time. The best rainmakers did not appear only when a process was live. They showed up years earlier with useful insight, relevant perspective, and consistency.


This still works. In many cases, it remains the foundation.


But it is no longer enough on its own.


Today, markets move faster, signal volume is higher, and competitive intensity is greater. The set of relevant companies is broader than any individual can track manually. The same is true for buyers. The same is true for outbound commercial opportunities. The firms that continue relying solely on artisanal coverage models will increasingly find themselves outflanked by teams that combine relationship skill with systematic market visibility.


### With Maywood


The key shift is from narrow human coverage to expanded, intelligence-driven coverage.


Maywood allows firms to keep the parts of dealmaking that matter most—judgment, positioning, trust, and relationship management—while dramatically increasing their ability to identify who matters, when they matter, and how to reach them.


That begins with scope.


A strong team may know the obvious 50 companies in a sector. A great team may know 200. But the true opportunity universe is often far larger: adjacent subsectors, overlooked founder-owned assets, recently recapitalized businesses, new private equity platforms, executives with relevant movement, emerging buyers, portfolio companies nearing strategic review, and companies exhibiting specific signs of transition.


The old model forces teams to shrink the market to fit what they can manually cover. The new model expands coverage to fit the actual market.


That changes the economics of origination.


## The Winning Formula Going Forward


### Step one: build scope around the right companies and the right people


Target identification, whether that is finding the right companies or people, is static—that does not cut it in an environment that is rapidly changing and evolving. Finding the right prospect needs to be dynamic, contextual, and tied to a specific objective. Maywood moves teams from broad market screens to targeted opportunity universes by combining company identification, role identification, industry context, and workflow-specific relevance.


Instead of building lists one company at a time, Maywood defines the ideal profile, maps the surrounding market, and identifies the specific people most likely to matter within each target. That changes list building from manual assembly into a strategic coverage exercise.


The immediate effect is simple: more shots on goal, with higher quality.


### Step two: identify indicators of timing


Knowing who matters is useful. Knowing when to engage is what creates leverage.


Historically, timing was inferred through experience. A banker who had covered an industry for 15 years might sense that a founder was getting tired, that a sponsor was approaching the end of a hold, or that a strategic buyer was ready to move after a change in leadership. These signals still exist. But many are now visible through a broader and more searchable set of data.


Timing indicators can include changes in executive leadership, acquisition activity, capital raises, portfolio age, hiring patterns, geographic expansion, management commentary, industry dislocation, regulatory shifts, product launches, cost pressure, margin compression, lender dynamics, or competitor actions. None of these alone guarantees action. But in aggregate, they can materially improve the probability that outreach happens at the right moment.


This is where most teams remain underpowered. They may track a few obvious signals, but not systematically, not across a sufficiently broad universe, and not simultaneously.


Maywood changes that by automatically monitoring markets with greater continuity and structure. Instead of waiting for a process to become visible, Maywood watches for the underlying conditions that often precede strategic action. Instead of relying on memory and chance, Maywood builds a proactive view of which companies and buyers are becoming relevant.


That is a major shift. It moves origination from reactive awareness to active readiness.


### Step three: map the relationship path, not just the target


A cold email to the wrong executive at the wrong moment, with no relevant angle, is noise. A well-timed note through the right path, grounded in genuine context, is a conversation.


Maywood turns relationship mapping into a repeatable capability. Instead of forcing bankers to reconstruct history from memory, email searches, and disconnected systems, the platform discovers prior touchpoints and best paths to introduction. Maywood then creates this roadmap for people and automatically gets them in front of the right person to unlock the opportunity.


Not every path will be warm. But every path can be more informed.


### Step four: tailor the approach to maximize response quality


It is amazing what high depth personalization can do to piquing interest and moving people along in a process. It shows that you care about this relationship, that you pay attention, and you are heavily invested in strengthening ties going forward. This is especially true when you are reaching out to someone about a new opportunity where you need to bring them into the funnel. In general, this is extremely difficult to scale—scattered notes in systems that do not talk to one another, countless relationships to keep track of, and outdated CRMs.


Maywood flips the script. Not only can Maywood leverage scattered internal and external data to personalize emails, but it also continuously A/B tests until it reaches optimal outreach. By letting Maywood continuously send across emails to prospects, firms now increase hit rates through the maximum level of personalization to pique interest and maintain warm relationships while self-upgrading with each incremental communication.


Instead of treating outreach as a pure volume game, Maywood enables firms to approach each company with more context: why this company matters, why this person matters, why now is relevant, and what angle is most likely to resonate.


## So What?


### A materially expanded universe of opportunities


The most obvious benefit of this model is scale.


When Maywood systematically identifies relevant companies, surfaces timing indicators, maps the best relationship path, and tailors the approach, the addressable universe of real opportunities expands dramatically.


That matters because most firms are still fishing in a relatively small pond. They go where their current coverage model allows them to go. Maywood significantly expands this horizon.


It enables firms to cover more sectors with more depth, identify more actionable opportunities within each sector. Instead of adding more people just to keep up with market sprawl, they can increase throughput through better intelligence and better orchestration.


But scale alone is not the real prize. The real prize is conversion.


### Better-informed outreach wins more often


Scale can happen using the spray and pray approach, but that will lead to low response rates and minimal interest. Once a firm scales their universe, they need to ensure that they build strong rapport and pique vested interest with their prospects through hyperpersonalization. Conversations start warmer. Messaging becomes more relevant. Follow-up becomes more precise. Meetings are more likely to happen. And when they do happen, teams show up with far better context.


That creates compounding effects. Better initial access leads to better credibility. Better credibility leads to stronger relationships. Stronger relationships lead to earlier awareness and better information. Over time, firms do not just increase volume. They improve market position.


This is especially important in an environment where everyone claims to be “data-driven,” but relatively few teams actually translate data into a repeatable go-to-market motion for dealmaking.


### Clear process orchestration becomes a competitive edge


Getting in front of the right person is not the end of the process. It is the beginning of a more complex sequence: follow-ups, internal coordination, relationship management, buyer identification, diligence preparation, process movement, and ultimately execution.


This is why point solutions do not solve the full problem.


Even if a team identifies the right target and books the right meeting, value is lost if the next steps are fragmented across inboxes, spreadsheets, inconsistent notes, and individual memory. Revenue leakage often happens because the process around leads are not orchestrated tightly enough.


Maywood matters here because it helps connect identification, timing, relationship intelligence, outreach, and workflow orchestration into one operating motion.

The future of winning top-line growth in dealmaking will belong to firms that combine three things:

- **Systematic expansion of scope:** Firms need broader, more dynamic visibility into companies, buyers, and people than traditional manual workflows allow.
- **Orchestration:** The market is too complex for intelligence to live in disconnected tools and individual memory. It has to translate into clear action.


Maywood sits at that intersection.


It helps firms identify the right companies and buyers, determine who matters within them, understand when engagement is most likely to work, map the best path to access, and coordinate the process that follows.
