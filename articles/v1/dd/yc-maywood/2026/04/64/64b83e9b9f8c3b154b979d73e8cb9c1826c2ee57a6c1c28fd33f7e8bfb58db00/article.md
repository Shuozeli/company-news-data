# Stop Using AI Like It’s 2023. Build the Outcomes That Will Define 2027.

Most firms still deploy AI with a 2023 mindset.


They buy copilots, test note-takers, automate isolated tasks, and celebrate faster output. The results are often real, but they are usually incremental. A team saves time on drafting. Research gets faster. Internal workflows become more efficient around the edges.


Then the harder question shows up: if AI is so powerful, why does the business still feel largely the same?


The answer is quite straightforward. Most firms applied AI to tasks when the larger opportunity was to improve the system around those tasks. They treated AI as a productivity layer for individuals rather than an operating layer for the business.


That approach made sense in the first wave. It will not define the winners in the next one.


By 2027, the firms that benefit most from AI will not be the ones that generate the most content or automate the most isolated actions. They will be the ones that use AI to improve execution, preserve context, and increase the number of important outcomes they can reliably produce.


That is the shift underway now. Most of the market still has not fully adjusted to it.


## The First Wave Made People Faster. The Next Wave Will Make Businesses Stronger.


The early AI playbook was built around individual acceleration. Write faster. Summarize faster. Research faster. Build first drafts faster. Reduce the time required to complete repetitive knowledge work.


That was a useful starting point. It was never the full prize.


Most businesses are constrained by how well the organization moves as a whole. They win or lose based on how information flows, how decisions are made, how context is preserved, how teams stay aligned, and how consistently the business can execute under pressure.


This is why so many AI deployments look impressive in a demo and underwhelming in production. The model performs well, but the workflow around it does not. Information still sits in too many systems, while important context still gets lost between people and processes. Managers still spend time reconciling fragmented activity across email, documents, spreadsheets, and systems of record.


AI did not fail in these environments. It was just applied too narrowly.


The real opportunity is larger. AI should improve how the business operates, not simply how quickly one person completes a task.


## Most Firms Optimized for Activity. The Winners Will Optimize for Outcomes.


This is the central distinction that will define the next phase of the market.


Activity is easy to measure, so firms naturally gravitate toward it. They count summaries generated, drafts produced, workflows automated, and hours saved. Those metrics are not meaningless, but they are often poor proxies for the outcomes that actually matter.


Businesses do not buy software to generate more internal motion. They buy software to improve revenue, capacity, conversion, speed to close, consistency, and decision quality. They buy it to reduce leakage in critical processes. They buy it to make fewer mistakes and carry less operational drag. They buy it to increase the number of important things the organization can do well at once.


Once AI becomes broadly available, faster generation by itself stops being a durable advantage. The value shifts to the system that can apply intelligence in the right place, at the right time, with the right context, inside a workflow that actually produces a business result.


That is why 2027 will reward firms that think in outcomes rather than outputs. The difference sounds subtle. It is not. It changes what gets built, what gets bought, and where advantage accumulates.


## The Next Moat Is Orchestration.


A large share of the market still behaves as if the core question is model quality. Better models do matter. Better reasoning, larger context windows, stronger multimodal performance, and more reliable agent behavior all create value.


None of those things, on their own, create control.


A better model inside a fragmented organization often produces more material to review, more surface area to manage, and more operational complexity to absorb. Intelligence without orchestration does not automatically improve execution. In many cases, it simply accelerates the creation of intermediate work.


The firms that win will solve a different problem. They will build systems that understand where work sits, what has already happened, what source material matters, who needs to act next, where risk is emerging, and how the current process compares to prior ones. They will connect intelligence to the flow of execution.


That is what orchestration means in practice. It means AI is not operating as an isolated assistant. It is embedded inside the process itself, helping the organization move with more consistency and less friction.


This is where the next layer of defensibility lives. Models will improve across the board. Orchestration remains much harder. It requires deep integration into how real work happens, how exceptions are handled, how decisions are made, and how context moves across systems and people. It also requires proactive work that abstracts away context switching for bankers themselves.


## Institutional Memory Will Separate the Leaders from Everyone Else.


Most organizations still lose an enormous amount of value every time a workflow ends.


Important context stays trapped in inboxes, meeting notes, trackers, ad hoc chats, and individual judgment calls. The next project begins without the full benefit of what the last one should have taught the business. Teams repeat work, revisit old questions, miss recurring patterns, and depend too heavily on whoever happens to remember the last time something similar happened.


That is not a small inefficiency. It is a structural weakness.


The firms that lead in 2027 will treat every workflow as an opportunity to strengthen the next one. They will capture what mattered, what changed, what stalled, which actions moved the process forward, where risk first surfaced, and what information proved decisive. Over time, that memory compounds.


Once it compounds, it becomes one of the most valuable assets in the business.


This is especially true in high-stakes, multi-step environments where execution quality determines revenue. In those settings, institutional memory is no longer a nice-to-have. It becomes part of the operating advantage. It improves judgment, reduces redundancy, speeds up decision-making, and allows the organization to handle more volume without a linear increase in chaos.


The market still underestimates how important this will become. Many firms are still buying AI as if the main goal is to complete the current task more cheaply. The firms that pull ahead will use AI to ensure that each workflow leaves the business stronger than it was before.


## The Unit of Value Is Shifting from the Task to the Process.


This is the broader category change underneath everything else.


The old unit of value was the task. Summarize this call. Draft this memo. Rewrite this email. Pull this research. Answer this question.


The new unit of value is the process. Run this workflow more effectively. Preserve context across the full cycle. Surface risk earlier. Keep teams aligned. Reduce drag between steps. Make the next execution stronger because of what the last one taught the system.


This is a more demanding standard. It is also far more valuable.


Traditional software organized data and created systems of record. Early AI tools increased the speed at which people interacted with those systems. The next generation of AI software will shape how the workflow itself is carried out. That is a much larger role. It reaches beyond assistance into coordination, continuity, and control.


The companies that understand this shift early will build very differently from the ones that do not. They will invest less energy in chasing one more point solution and more energy in building an operating layer that spans the full process.


## Investment Banking Makes This Shift Especially Clear.


This is one of the reasons investment banking is such an important proving ground for the next generation of AI systems.


Banking is not primarily constrained by a shortage of smart people or a shortage of content generation. It is constrained by fragmented execution. Critical information lives across decks, emails, buyer conversations, diligence trackers, data rooms, spreadsheets, and internal judgment. Momentum gets lost. Context breaks. Teams spend time reassembling the state of the process instead of advancing it.


This is why so much value in banking is tied to coordination. The bottleneck is rarely one individual task in isolation. It is the ability to move an entire transaction forward with precision, speed, and consistency from preparation through close.


That is also why the long-term opportunity for AI in banking is not just drafting materials or producing answers more quickly. It is coordinating the process around materials, buyers, diligence, source traceability, and institutional learning. It is increasing throughput. It is reducing the number of places a live process can degrade. It is helping firms close more deals with less operational friction.


The same pattern will emerge in every complex, high-value workflow category. The organizations that gain the most from AI will be the ones that redesign execution around it.


## By 2027, the Market Will Be Divided in a More Meaningful Way.


The real divide will not be between firms that use AI and firms that do not.


It will be between firms that still think of AI as a collection of tools and firms that treat AI as part of the operating layer of the business.


The first group will have more assistants, more pilots, and more generated material. They may have lower labor costs on certain tasks and faster turnaround in specific areas. But they will still run critical workflows through fragmented systems, manual coordination, and weak institutional memory.


The second group will operate differently. They will preserve context across processes, connect intelligence to execution, and make each workflow strengthen the next one. Their advantage will show up in throughput, consistency, visibility, and business outcomes. They will not simply move faster at isolated moments. They will run better systems.


That is the difference that will matter.


## The Right Strategic Question Has Changed.


Too many firms still ask what AI can do for a person sitting at a desk. That question defined the first phase of adoption, and it produced useful products.


It is no longer the most important question.


The better question is what the business should become when intelligence is embedded into how work is coordinated, how decisions are informed, and how knowledge compounds over time.


That question leads to a very different roadmap. It pushes firms to think less about adding another tool and more about redesigning execution. It forces them to ask where context breaks, where momentum dies, where redundancy accumulates, and where a lack of memory quietly taxes the organization every day.


The firms that answer those questions well will shape the next category leaders.


By 2027, the advantage will not come from having experimented with AI earliest or talked about it the most. It will come from building systems that produce stronger outcomes because intelligence is deeply integrated into the way the business runs.


That is what the next phase of AI adoption actually demands.


The firms that recognize it now will build differently. The rest will spend the next few years buying faster tools for slower systems.
