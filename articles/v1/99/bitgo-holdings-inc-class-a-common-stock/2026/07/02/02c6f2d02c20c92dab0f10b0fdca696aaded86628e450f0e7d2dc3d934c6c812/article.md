# How Many Bitcoins Are Lost? Estimates, Causes, and What It Means for Supply

An estimated 2.3 to 4 million bitcoins, or roughly 11 to 18 percent of the 21 million supply cap, are believed to be permanently lost.


However, it’s impossible to be certain how many bitcoins are lost; the blockchain doesn’t flag them. Analysts make inferences from dormant addresses, known incidents such as discarded hard drives or forgotten passwords, and unspent transaction output (UTXO) age.


Blockchain transactions are irreversible, and if one loses access to their[private keys](https://www.bitgo.com/products/cold-wallets/) , their coins are gone forever. Proper[custodial arrangements](https://www.bitgo.com/products/qualified-custody/) and inheritance protocols are key to ensuring holders do not become unintentional donors to the rest of the network.


In this article, we’ll discuss how many bitcoins are lost, the market impact, and how investors can protect their digital assets.

## What Counts as a Lost Bitcoin?


Bitcoin operates with a pair of cryptographic keys, one private, one public. And unlike fiat money in a bank, if someone loses access to their keys, those coins are gone forever. There is no central authority to reset a password or retrieve lost funds, and guessing a private key without any hints is computationally impossible with today's technology. Thus, lost bitcoin sits in its address, nearly indistinguishable from long-term holders, entombed on the blockchain until someone guesses or stumbles on the key.


There are three types of “lost” bitcoin:


-


**Lost:** Still exists on-chain, but the owner likely lost their keys and can no longer access them.


-


**Dormant:** Hasn’t moved in a long time, but may still be spendable. Often indistinguishable from lost bitcoin.


-


**Burned:** Deliberately removed from circulation by users who send bitcoin to publicly visible “burn wallets” that have no known private key.


The trouble with estimating how many bitcoins are lost forever is we can only make educated guesses. Some analysts say ~2 million are lost, while others reach as high as 5.6 million by relying on dormant bitcoin estimates.


## How Analysts Estimate Lost Bitcoin


Since the blockchain doesn’t explicitly mark coins as lost, analysts make observations using indirect methods.


One of the most common approaches is dormant address analysis, where wallets that haven’t transacted coins for five years or more are flagged as potentially lost.


For example, Unchained Finance put together a fantastic[write-up](https://www.unchained.com/blog/hodl-waves-1) detailing the “unspent transaction output” (UTXO) of bitcoin over time.


In plain English, they charted how long it’s been since a particular amount of bitcoin was last transacted.


According to their analysis, it’s been three or more years since ~43% of all bitcoin in circulation was transacted. Are those dormant addresses owned by buy-and-hold investors? Or are they lost forever?


It’s difficult to be certain how many bitcoins are lost, but analysts tend to consider coins that haven’t moved for at least five years likely to be out of circulation forever.


On-chain analytics firms like[Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/) and[Glassnode](https://glassnode.com/) refine these estimates by combining clustering algorithms, transaction history, and UTXO age analysis. Their models group addresses and assess the likelihood of loss, but always include uncertainty, since inactivity could also reflect intentional long-term holding.


While about ~20 million BTC have been mined, the effective circulating supply is likely between 15.8 and 17.5 million.


NOTE: These numbers will always be approximations, given the decentralized and pseudonymous nature of the network.


## Famous Cases of Lost Bitcoin


The most prominent example is the roughly 1.1 million BTC mined by Bitcoin's creator, Satoshi Nakamoto, which have never been moved. Since these coins have remained untouched since 2009-2010, these coins are widely assumed to be permanently out of circulation.


Any hint of them moving, or a return of Satoshi in general, would be a global industry event.


James Howells is likely the most famous case of real-world loss due to human error. After discarding a hard drive with 8,000 BTC in 2013, he offered to fully fund the landfill excavation process, as well as share 25% of recovered bitcoin with the relevant local city council, but was denied. A judge recently dismissed his lawsuit seeking permission to excavate.


[Stefan Thomas’](https://www.businessinsider.com/bitcoin-owner-who-lost-password-made-peace-potentially-huge-loss-2021-1) is another famous case. He lost the password to his IronKey, a brand of ultra-secure hardware-encrypted USB flash drives, with 7,000+ BTC. Ironically, despite being a software programmer with the inclination to invest in an IronKey in the first place, he ran into trouble after losing the physical piece of paper on which he had written his password.


## Bitcoin’s Invisible Burn: Ancient Coins vs. New Issuance


According to a 2025[report](https://fidelitydigitalassets.com/research-and-insights/increasing-impact-bitcoins-ancient-supply?ccsource=owned_social_impact_bitcoin_ancient_supply_x) by Fidelity Digital Assets, using data from Glassnode, bitcoin's "ancient supply," coins that have not moved onchain in over 10 years, is now growing faster than new coins are being mined. More than 566 BTC per day are aging into this category, while miners produce only 450 BTC per day following the 2024 halving.


Reasons for this dormancy may include lost private keys, deliberate long-term holding,[institutional cold storage](https://www.bitgo.com/products/cold-wallets/) , or early adopters permanently exiting the market. Regardless of the cause, this phenomenon creates a striking imbalance.


The chart above highlights the changing dynamics between new bitcoin issuance and the growth of ancient supply. Since 2019, daily issuance has steadily declined through halving cycles, while the reactivation of long-dormant "ancient" coins has gradually increased. By 2025, the flow of ancient supply entering circulation was comparable to the daily issuance of new coins.


## Why Lost Bitcoin Matters for Price and Scarcity


Lost coins amplify Bitcoin’s scarcity premium.


It is thought that millions of bitcoin have likely been lost through forgotten keys, discarded hardware, and dormant wallets leaving today’s effective circulating supply between 15.8 and 17.5 million BTC, not the 21 million cap.


As new issuance slows with each halving, the effect of these lost coins compounds, pushing Bitcoin toward greater long-term scarcity.


The trend underscores the diminishing role of miner rewards in expanding Bitcoin's supply.


With issuance shrinking and a growing share of supply effectively locked or re-emerging only sporadically, Bitcoin's scarcity dynamics are increasingly driven by wallet behavior and long-term holder decisions. The status of dormant coins can change suddenly if moved, but the overall pattern reinforces Bitcoin's monetary policy design and the importance of supply-side constraints in shaping its value proposition.


For investors, this matters because price discovery works alongside only Bitcoin's effective supply; not the cap designed by Satoshi Nakamoto.


When demand rises against a pool of coins that’s meaningfully smaller than the headline figure, the same buying pressure produces a sharper response. All else equal, fewer coins in circulation produce higher prices than would otherwise exist.


## Holder Mortality and Future Losses


Legal and[estate planning](https://www.bitgo.com/solutions/estates/) experts are concerned that millions of coins are becoming inaccessible due to the deaths of holders who didn't plan ahead. Inheritance planning was not a high priority when Bitcoin was trading at a fraction of today’s prices, because not everyone expected it to become a trillion-dollar asset class. Fast forward to today; having a plan is mandatory.


Experts in the crypto space have long warned that the situation is worsening. A 2020 survey by the Cremation Institute,[highlighted in Bitcoin Magazine](https://bitcoinmagazine.com/culture/what-happens-to-my-bitcoin-when-i-die) , found that while nearly 90% of crypto holders were concerned about what would happen to their assets upon death, only a small fraction had made formal plans for their inheritance. This disconnect between awareness and preparation highlights a vulnerability for long-term Bitcoin accessibility.


Meanwhile, Bitcoin's new issuance is decreasing with each halving. By 2028, Bitcoin's annual issuance will drop again to ~82,000 BTC per year. If just 0.5% of current holders were to lose access annually, that would equate to roughly 95,000 BTC disappearing from circulation each


year. At a 1% annual loss rate, the number rises to roughly 190,000 BTC, more than double Bitcoin's post-2028 yearly issuance.


For investors using ETFs or regulated custodians, estate planning is straightforward.


For those holding private keys directly, the risk is different. If your family cannot locate or unlock your wallet, your coins are gone forever. Planning ahead ensures your wealth transfers securely rather than disappearing.


## How to Avoid Losing Your Bitcoin


Protecting bitcoin from permanent loss comes down to choosing who and how one manages their keys, and executing it well.


For those who self-custody, the following best practices are clear:


-


**Multi-Signature Wallets:** Multifactor authentication protects from cybercriminals by ensuring no single point of failure can compromise an account.


-


**Geographically Separated Backups:** Those with significant digital assets often store keys on “cold” hardware devices that never connect to the internet. Keeping backups in geographically distinct locations further protects against natural disaster, theft, or loss.


-


**Succession Planning:** Documenting where and how to access assets is crucial. If one passes away unexpectedly, there is no central authority for next-of-kin to appeal to.


The alternative is qualified custody, where a regulated custodian manages key infrastructure, compliance, and continuity. Estate planning is considerably more straightforward when custodians hold the keys.


The choice is simple. Plan now, or risk making a donation to other holders unintentionally.

## FAQs


### What counts as a "lost" Bitcoin?


Lost bitcoin are coins that can no longer be traded because access to their private keys is gone. The coins remain visible on the blockchain, but are permanently unusable due to forgotten passwords, discarded hard drives, or deceased holders who left no succession plan.


### Can lost Bitcoin be recovered?


Rarely. If a private key is truly gone, no one can recover the coins. Partial recovery is sometimes possible through password-cracking devices or data recovery from damaged hardware, but success is unlikely.


### How do analysts estimate the number of lost Bitcoin?


Analysts begin by studying on-chain data for coins that haven’t moved in five years or more. Then, firms combine this signal with known lost addresses, such as Satoshi Nakamoto’s, to produce estimates. Many estimate that between 3 to 4 million bitcoins are lost.


### What are "ancient" Bitcoin and why do they matter?


“Ancient” bitcoin are coins that haven’t moved in ten years or more. Many are presumed lost, but some belong to early holders who may still control their keys.


They matter because any sudden movement of ancient coins can shift effective supply, and market sentiment, overnight.


### How does the Bitcoin halving affect circulating supply?


Roughly every four years, the halving cuts the rate of new bitcoin issuance in half. Each cycle slows the pace at which new supply enters circulation. Separately, coins that are lost or otherwise inaccessible continue to make up a growing share of the total supply over time, since that supply may never return to circulation.


### What happens to my Bitcoin when I die?


Without a documented succession plan, your bitcoin likely becomes permanently inaccessible. It will still be visible on the blockchain, but heirs can’t recover keys they don’t have.


### How can institutions and high-net-worth holders prevent loss of Bitcoin?


Many prudent institutions and individuals rely on qualified custodians, which provide institutional-grade key management, security, insurance, and regulatory compliance. For those who self-custody, multi-signature authentication, distributed key backups, and formalized succession planning can reduce points of failure. The common thread is to remove any one person as a critical dependency.
