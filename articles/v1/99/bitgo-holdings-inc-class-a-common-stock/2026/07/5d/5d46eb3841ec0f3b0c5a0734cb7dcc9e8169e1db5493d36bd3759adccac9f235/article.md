# Stablecoins and Crypto Serve Different Jobs

## Key Takeaways


-


Stablecoins and crypto assets increasingly occupy different roles on institutional balance sheets.


-


Stablecoins support settlement, payments, and treasury workflows that depend on predictable liquidity.


-


Bitcoin and other crypto assets are generally managed as exposure assets, with controls aligned to investment objectives and risk tolerance.


-


Hidden risk may emerge when institutions apply the same assumptions to movement assets and exposure assets.


-


Institutions often need both, supported by infrastructure capable of applying different controls to different jobs.


Institutions have spent years discussing digital assets as though they belong to a single category. Recent market behavior suggests the distinction within the asset class may be more important than the similarities.


During the first quarter of 2026, broader crypto markets declined sharply while stablecoin supply continued to[grow](https://blog.cex.io/ecosystem/q1-2026-stablecoin-report-35459) . Stablecoins also accounted for roughly[three-quarters](https://blog.cex.io/ecosystem/q1-2026-stablecoin-report-35459) of crypto trading activity during the period. Rather than signaling a wholesale retreat from digital assets, the trend highlighted the continued importance of liquidity during periods when institutions became more selective about taking market exposure.


For treasury teams, that behavior reinforces a practical reality. Assets that share the same underlying technology may serve very different purposes once they reach the balance sheet. Stablecoins increasingly support the day-to-day movement of value within institutional operations. Treasury teams use them when liquidity needs to be available, obligations need to be met, and funds need to move without introducing additional market exposure.


Bitcoin, Ether, and other crypto assets are managed differently. They are typically held as investment positions, with decisions shaped by an institution's objectives, risk tolerance, and broader allocation strategy.


Understanding the role an asset is expected to perform influences everything that follows, from governance and reporting requirements to how institutions move capital and manage risk.


## Stablecoins Handle the Cash Job


Institutions do not manage cash primarily for its return potential. They manage it so obligations can be met without disruption, whether that means settling trades, moving funds between counterparties, or ensuring liquidity is available when activity spans multiple markets and time zones. The challenge is less about how much cash an organization has and more about whether it can access and move that cash when it needs to.


Traditional treasury operations have developed around those constraints. Firms maintain buffers across accounts, prefund certain activities, and structure processes around banking schedules because timing matters just as much as availability.[Stablecoins](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/the-stable-door-opens-how-tokenized-cash-enables-next-gen-payments) offer another way to address that same problem by allowing dollar-denominated liquidity to remain inside digital asset workflows rather than moving in and out of them as payment windows open and close.


Changing the infrastructure may also change how institutions manage liquidity. Capital that once sat idle across accounts to accommodate settlement windows and banking schedules may become easier to redeploy when access to funds is less constrained by those timing considerations.[Treasury teams](https://www.bitgo.com/resources/blog/stablecoin-treasury-management/) still have the same responsibilities, but they may have more flexibility in how they meet them.


Periods of market stress often make those priorities more visible. Institutions may scale back exposure to directional positions, but they still need to move money, manage liquidity, and meet obligations. The continued use of stablecoins during those periods reflects the persistence of the underlying need. The work changes very little, even when market sentiment does.


The[GENIUS Act](https://www.richmondfed.org/banking/banker_resources/news_flash/2025/20251118_genius_act) reflects the standards that emerge when institutions begin relying on stablecoins in that way. Expectations around reserves, redemption rights, and supervision become more important when those assets support day-to-day operations through changing market conditions. Rather than creating an entirely new set of priorities, the framework formalizes many of the practices institutions were already looking for as stablecoins became more deeply embedded in treasury activity.


## Crypto Handles the Exposure Job


Institutions hold Bitcoin, Ether, and other digital assets because they expect those positions to contribute to the balance sheet over time. Some treat them as strategic reserves, while others include them within broader investment mandates. In each case, establishing a position is also a decision about how much exposure the institution is willing to take.


That decision rarely stays fixed forever. A position that once represented a small allocation can grow substantially during a market rally. A drawdown can raise questions about whether the original sizing still makes sense. Institutions need a way to evaluate whether positions remain appropriate as conditions change and how those changes affect the broader balance sheet.


Those discussions often involve more than the investment team. Finance leaders, boards, and risk committees may all want visibility into concentration, performance, and how digital assets fit within broader portfolio objectives. Oversight helps institutions make deliberate decisions about exposure instead of revisiting them only when markets force the issue.


Custody and reporting support that process. Clear authority over asset movement, approval workflows, secure long-term storage, and ongoing visibility into position activity help institutions implement the decisions they have made while maintaining an understanding of how exposure evolves over time.


Managing exposure is rarely a set-it-and-forget-it exercise. Positions may grow, shrink, or take on a different level of significance as markets move and institutional priorities evolve. The objective is to make sure digital assets continue serving their intended purpose rather than allowing market movements or changing conditions to dictate the role those positions play on the balance sheet.


## Both Support Liquidity, But in Different Ways


Financing arrangements are not new to institutional teams. Firms have long used collateral to secure obligations, support trading activity, and maintain access to liquidity. Stablecoins and crypto assets can both be used in those arrangements, which can make the activity itself feel familiar even when the assets involved behave very differently.


Stablecoins are often valued in these settings because they bring a degree of consistency to the process. If a stablecoin is posted as[collateral](https://www.bitgo.com/resources/blog/stablecoins-as-collateral/) today, institutions generally expect the value supporting the arrangement to remain close to where it started tomorrow. Stablecoins are also relatively easy to move between venues and counterparties, allowing teams to focus on the financing activity itself rather than frequent changes in the collateral backing it.


[Crypto collateral](https://www.bitgo.com/resources/blog/crypto-collateral-management-institutions/) changes the experience. Institutions may choose to pledge those assets because doing so provides access to financing without giving up the potential benefit of future appreciation. The financing arrangement may be exactly the same, but the value supporting it can move significantly over short periods of time. Teams may need to decide whether additional collateral should be posted, whether positions should be adjusted, and who has the authority to approve those decisions before timing becomes a constraint.


Those moments often reveal whether a process was built with the underlying assets in mind. Procedures that work well when collateral values remain relatively stable may come under pressure when collateral itself becomes another variable that needs to be monitored and managed. The issue is not that one approach is right and the other is wrong. It is whether the people, timelines, and controls surrounding the arrangement reflect the behavior of the assets supporting it.


Hidden risk often emerges when familiar processes are built around the wrong assumptions. Institutions do not necessarily need different financing activities for stablecoins and crypto, but they may need different expectations about how those activities operate once markets begin to move.


## Why Institutions Need Infrastructure Built for Both


At this point, the challenge is no longer deciding whether an institution needs stablecoins or crypto assets. Many already use both. The challenge is supporting assets that perform different jobs while applying the controls each one requires.


Stablecoin activity depends on capabilities tied to issuance, redemption, reserve custody, and settlement workflows. Exposure assets benefit from policy controls, approval structures, secure long-term custody, and reporting that treats those holdings like other risk positions on the balance sheet. The requirements are different because the jobs are different.


Institutions do not necessarily need separate vendors or disconnected operating environments to support those activities. They benefit from infrastructure capable of applying different operating models within a single platform, allowing movement assets and exposure assets to coexist without forcing identical workflows onto both.

The same balance sheet may rely on assets designed to move value and assets intended to create it. Infrastructure is better positioned to support both functions when it recognizes the difference, applying the appropriate controls to each while allowing institutions to operate within a consistent environment.


## FAQs


### What are stablecoins used for?


Stablecoins are commonly used for settlement, payments, treasury operations, and maintaining operating liquidity onchain. Institutions use them when they need to move or hold dollar-denominated value without taking direct exposure to crypto price movements.


### How are stablecoins different from crypto?


Stablecoins are generally used to move and hold value, while crypto assets such as Bitcoin and Ether are more often held for exposure, appreciation, or collateral purposes. The distinction comes down to the job each asset is expected to perform on the balance sheet.


### Why do institutions use stablecoins?


Institutions use stablecoins because they provide dollar-denominated liquidity that can support settlement, payments, and treasury activity without relying entirely on traditional banking schedules. The objective is operational efficiency rather than investment return.


### Can crypto be used as collateral?


Yes. Institutions may pledge crypto as collateral to access financing while retaining exposure to the underlying asset. Because collateral values can change quickly, those arrangements often require more active monitoring and clearly defined response procedures.


### Should institutions choose stablecoins or crypto?


Many institutions use both because they solve different problems. Stablecoins support settlement and operating liquidity, while crypto assets are typically used for exposure, strategic reserves, and certain collateral arrangements. The more useful question is which job the institution is trying to accomplish.


### Should stablecoins and crypto be managed on the same platform?


Institutions do not necessarily need separate systems for assets performing different jobs. The key is using infrastructure that supports different controls and workflows depending on whether the objective is settlement and operating liquidity, or exposure management, with financing arrangements drawing on capabilities from both.
